	\section*{\textsc{preliminary statement}}
	
	\begin{paras}
		
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item This case is about one error and a county's decision to protect it rather than correct it. On September 18, 2024, a single Washington County deputy misidentified Plaintiff---a Black woman---as a suspect she did not resemble in any respect other than race, and arrested her without a warrant and without probable cause. What followed was not a series of unrelated mistakes. As the error surfaced, each Washington County actor who touched it---the Sheriff's Office, the District Attorney, the Circuit Court, Community Corrections, and County Human Resources---acted to insulate the original error from correction rather than to remedy it. The fourteen claims that follow are therefore not fourteen separate grievances. They are one constitutional error and the county's successive, documented efforts to contain it---the definition of a municipal custom under \textit{Monell v.\ Dep't of Soc.\ Servs.}, 436 U.S.\ 658 (1978).
		
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item This Complaint does not ask the Court to credit Plaintiff's word over the county's. It rests on the county's own records. Deputy Rambin's own supplemental report, filed December 27, 2024, concedes in writing that the person he arrested was not the suspect. The State's own discovery production contains a probable cause order that is entirely blank---no finding, no signature, no date---documentary proof that no neutral magistrate ever authorized Plaintiff's detention. The Washington County Trial Court Administrator confirmed in writing that no valid Presiding Judge Order authorized the grand jury that indicted Plaintiff. And Defendant Mendes's own denial letter states, in writing, that Plaintiff's public records request was refused because of her associations and her interest in providing legal assistance to others. At each step, the government's own paper---not Plaintiff's characterization of it---establishes the violation. Nor does Plaintiff stand alone in describing this system: in \textit{Betschart v.\ State of Oregon}, No.\ 23-2270 (9th Cir.\ May 31, 2024), the Ninth Circuit held that these same defendants---the Washington County Circuit Court judges and the Washington County Sheriff---maintained a system that deprived pretrial defendants of their constitutional rights, and that federal intervention was warranted notwithstanding \textit{Younger}.
		
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item This action arises from a coordinated pattern of constitutional
		violations by Washington County officials against Plaintiff AshLe'
		Penn. Those violations began on September 18, 2024, when Washington
		County Deputy Rambin arrested Plaintiff without a warrant and without
		probable cause, having misidentified her as another suspect. The two
		women shared no identifying characteristics other than race. Rambin's
		own supplemental report, filed December 27, 2024, acknowledges the
		misidentification. The arrest was the product of racial profiling:
		Plaintiff was seized because she was a Black woman in proximity to a
		location associated with another Black woman, not because any
		individualized probable cause existed as to her.
		
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item Plaintiff brings this action pursuant to 42 U.S.C.\ \sect\ 1983
		for violations of her rights under the First, Fourth, and Fourteenth
		Amendments to the United States Constitution, seeking compensatory
		damages, punitive damages against individual Defendants, declaratory
		relief, and injunctive relief.
		
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item \textit{Younger} abstention does not bar this action.
		\textit{Younger v.\ Harris}, 401 U.S.\ 37, 43--54 (1971).
		Abstention is a narrow, prudential limit on authority that Congress affirmatively conferred---not a bar to it. In \sect\ 1983 itself, Congress expressly authorized the federal courts to enjoin state-court proceedings to vindicate federal rights: \sect\ 1983 is an ``expressly authorized'' exception to the federal anti-injunction statute, 28 U.S.C.\ \S\ 2283. \textit{Mitchum v.\ Foster}, 407 U.S.\ 225, 242--43 (1972). The \textit{Mitchum} Court grounded that authority in its recognition that ``federal injunctive relief against a state court proceeding can, in some circumstances, be essential to prevent great, immediate, and irreparable loss of a person's constitutional rights,'' citing \textit{Ex parte Young}, 209 U.S.\ 123 (1908), and \textit{Dombrowski v.\ Pfister}, 380 U.S.\ 479 (1965)---the same authorities on which Plaintiff relies here. \textit{Mitchum}, 407 U.S.\ at 242. Congress created that federal forum precisely because state tribunals would sometimes fail to enforce federal rights ``by reason of prejudice, passion, neglect, intolerance or otherwise.'' \textit{Monroe v.\ Pape}, 365 U.S.\ 167, 174--80 (1961). And that forum is not conditioned on first pursuing state relief: ``[t]he federal remedy is supplementary to the state remedy, and the state remedy need not be sought and refused before the federal remedy is invoked.'' \textit{Id.}\ at 183. A \sect\ 1983 plaintiff need not exhaust state remedies at all. \textit{Patsy v.\ Bd.\ of Regents}, 457 U.S.\ 496 (1982). \textit{Younger} did not repeal this authority; it channels its exercise. \textit{Younger} is inapplicable on five independent grounds.
		
	\end{paras}
	
	\begin{subparas}
		
		\item \textbf{No legitimate state proceeding.} \textit{Younger} presupposes a state court proceeding conducted
		pursuant to lawful authority and subject matter jurisdiction.  Article VII (Amended), Section 5 of the Oregon Constitution is what creates the grand jury. It is not a creature of statute — the grand jury exists because the constitution says it does, and it operates under the conditions the constitution sets. authority of a grand jury is a constitutional question, not just an administrative one.  The court in \textit{State v. Gortmaker} later confirmed and extended this: constitutional challenges to grand jury empanelment are cognizable independent of statutory challenges, because the grand jury procedure is constitutionally created and cannot be waived by statute. The grand jury's existence in Oregon is authorized by court order — specifically, the Presiding Judge Order ("PJO"). ORS \S132.120 is the mechanism: a grand jury's term ends when it ends, and if the presiding judge wants to continue it or empanel a new one, there must be an order entered of record. That is not a bureaucratic formality. It is the mechanism by which the court exercises its constitutional authority over the grand jury. Without a valid PJO, there is no court authorization, and without court authorization, there is no lawfully constituted grand jury.The
		indictment in Case No.\ 24CR49043 was returned by a grand jury on
		January 14, 2025, during a period when no valid Presiding Judge Order
		(PJO) authorized the grand jury's existence---PJO 325 expired
		January 3, 2025; PJO 350 did not take effect until January 31, 2025.
		The Washington County Trial Court Administrator confirmed this gap in
		writing. Under \textit{State v.\ Lawrence}, 12 Or.\ 297 (1885), a
		grand jury operating without lawful authority cannot issue a valid
		true bill. The indictment is void \textit{ab initio}. The
		Washington County Circuit Court has not acquired subject matter
		jurisdiction over this matter, and there is no legitimate state
		proceeding to which this Court should defer.
		\textit{Kugler v.\ Helfant}, 421 U.S.\ 117, 124--25 (1975).
		
		\item \textbf{Bad faith, harassment, and extraordinary circumstances.}
		\textit{Younger} abstention is "an extraordinary and narrow exception" to the general rule that a federal court's obligation to hear and decide a case is "virtually unflagging." Betschart v. State of Oregon, No. 23-2270, slip op. at 17 (9th Cir. May 31, 2024) (quoting Cook v. Harding, 879 F.3d 1035, 1038 (9th Cir. 2018)); Sprint Commc'ns, Inc. v. Jacobs, 571 U.S. 69, 77 (2013). Even when all four Younger factors are met, abstention is nevertheless inappropriate "where there exist other 'extraordinary circumstances in which the necessary irreparable injury can be shown even in the absence of the usual prerequisites of bad faith and harassment.'" Betschart, slip op. at 17 (quoting Kugler v. Helfant, 421 U.S. 117, 124 (1975)). The irreparable harm exception is an independent basis for federal intervention regardless of whether the Younger factors are met. Id. (citing Page v. King, 932 F.3d 898, 904 (9th Cir. 2019); Arevalo v. Hennessy, 882 F.3d 763, 766 (9th Cir. 2018)). "If meeting the preliminary four-factor test precluded application of the exception, there would be no exception at all." \textit{Betschart}, slip op.\ at 17.
		
		\textit{Betschart} is not merely persuasive authority---it was
		litigated against Washington County Sheriff Patrick Garrett and
		the Washington County Circuit Court Judges in their official
		capacities, the same Defendants here. \textit{Id.}, slip op.\ at
		1. The Ninth Circuit held that Washington County's failure to
		provide counsel to pretrial defendants constituted extraordinary
		circumstances warranting federal intervention notwithstanding
		
		\textit{Younger}. \textit{Id.}\ at 17--18.
		The majority's rebuke of the dissent is equally instructive:
		``The dissent's unbounded approach is an ode to classic judicial overreach.'' \textit{Id.}\ at 19. The Ninth Circuit thus confirmed that the extraordinary-circumstances exception is not a narrow escape hatch to be read out of existence by a court uncomfortable with federal intervention---it is a structural safeguard that this Court is obligated to apply when the conditions that justify it are present.
		They are present here.
		
		The specific bad faith predicates are: 
		(a) Rambin's own December 27,
		2024 supplemental report states in writing that the September 17
		suspect was not Plaintiff---the arresting officer confessed in a
		contemporaneous official document that probable cause never existed,
		and prosecution continued anyway; 
		(b) the January 14, 2025 grand jury
		operated during the PJO gap, rendering the indictment void and the
		prosecution incapable of producing a valid conviction on that
		instrument. These predicates, specifically, establish that the prosecution against Plaintiff is maintained without hope of a valid conviction. \textit{Dombrowski v.\ Pfister}, 380 U.S.\ 479, 490 (1965); \textit{Baffert v.\ California Horse Racing Bd.}, 332 F.3d 613, 621 (9th Cir.\ 2003) (prosecution brought without reasonable expectation of obtaining a valid conviction and motivated by retaliatory or harassing purpose). 
		(c) Rambin's December 27 report documents that he directed Target employees to compile all prior incidents involving Plaintiff after conceding his false arrest of Plaintiff as he believed her to be another suspect who'd been in the same store the night before committing theft. The night of Plaintiff's arrest, Rambin was dispatched to retrieve the surveillance evidence from loss prevention officer Sarah Fillis' call to dispatch of a "cold theft". Rambin appears not to have reviewed the footage until nearly 100 days after Plaintiff's arrest. Upon coming upon the knowledge that he'd misidentified Plaintiff, he returned to Target and directed the other grand jury witness (who was not present at the time of Plaintiff's arrest on September 18, 2024) to compile video footage of Plaintiff as he stated he would document the two thefts under different numbers and use the newly compiled video footage to manufacture a new set of facts for prosecution and quietly substitute the initial theft charge with the new set of facts---the exact pattern of multiple manufactured prosecutorial actions identified in \textit{Dombrowski}, 380 U.S.\ at 490.  
		
		(d) the Mendes denial letter cited Plaintiff's
		``interest in providing legal assistance to convicted perpetrators'' as the basis for denying her public records request, demonstrating
		that separate departments of Washington County government were actively using information about Plaintiff's	First Amendment activities in making adverse decisions against her,
		satisfying \textit{Coszalter v.\ City of Salem}, 320 F.3d 968, 977
		(9th Cir.\ 2003); and 
		(e) Plaintiff sought appellate review by filing an emergency petition for extraordinary relief on July 23, 2025 and a notice of appeal on July 24, 2025. The Court of Appeals dismissed both for lack of appellate jurisdiction on August 15, 2025, confirming that there was no state remedy.
		
	\end{subparas}
	\begin{paras}
		\item As a third and independent ground, \textit{Younger} abstention is inapplicable because the prosecution rests on a detention that was never judicially authorized. \textit{Younger} and its comity rationale presuppose a state court proceeding conducted pursuant to lawful authority over a lawfully detained defendant. \textit{Younger v.\ Harris}, 401 U.S.\ 37, 43--44 (1971). Where the defendant's detention was never validated by a neutral magistrate as required by \textit{Gerstein}, the 'adequate state forum' premise of \textit{Younger} is undermined. The state court in 24CR49043 has exercised jurisdiction over Plaintiff's liberty for more than 21 months without any judicial officer ever having made the constitutionally required probable cause determination. The blank probable cause order in the State's own discovery production---every field empty, no signature, no date, no finding---is documentary proof that the proceeding lacks the lawful foundation that \textit{Younger} comity presupposes. A federal court does not owe deference to a state proceeding that has operated outside constitutional authority from its inception.
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item As a fourth and independent ground, \textit{Younger} abstention is inapplicable because the state proceeding has been structurally engineered to prevent any constitutional question from ever reaching appellate review, satisfying both the bad-faith exception and the ``capable of repetition, yet evading review'' doctrine. \textit{Southern Pacific Terminal Co.\ v.\ ICC}, 219 U.S.\ 498, 515 (1911). The mechanism is two-layered. First, the OPDC placeholder attorney of record creates the appearance of constitutional compliance on the face of the docket while routing all court notices away from Plaintiff; the state simultaneously denies Plaintiff counsel and requires counsel as a condition of self-representation, and Stabler's false \textit{Roberts} objection blocks the state's own remedy. The constitutional violation is therefore self-concealing on the record---a reviewing court examining the docket sees an attorney of record and no apparent deprivation. Second, Plaintiff's demurrer and motion to quash---which present the constitutional questions directly---have been pending without ruling for more than twenty-one months. Oregon law bars interlocutory appeal of such rulings. ORS 138.035. The Court of Appeals confirmed on August 15, 2025 that no appellate jurisdiction exists. The trial court therefore controls the clock: by declining to rule, it keeps the constitutional questions permanently off appellate radar until conviction, at which point the harm will have been fully consummated. \textit{Younger} was never designed to immunize a proceeding that has been structured to defeat the very review that \textit{Younger} assumes will occur. \textit{Betschart v.\ State of Oregon}, No.\ 23-2270, slip op.\ at 18--19 (9th Cir.\ May 31, 2024) (extraordinary-circumstances exception operates independently of the four-factor test; \textit{Younger} does not apply where the state proceeding itself forecloses adequate constitutional review).
		
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item As a fifth and independent ground, \textit{Younger} abstention is inapplicable because the state tribunals adjudicating Plaintiff's matters are disqualified by bias, and a biased tribunal is not the competent, neutral state forum whose existence \textit{Younger} presupposes. Where the state adjudicator is ``incompetent by reason of bias to adjudicate the issues pending before it,'' the ``predicate for a \textit{Younger v.\ Harris} dismissal [is] lacking,'' and the federal court ``need not defer.'' \textit{Gibson v.\ Berryhill}, 411 U.S.\ 564, 577--579 (1973); \textit{see Kugler v.\ Helfant}, 421 U.S.\ 117, 124--25 (1975) (identifying \textit{Gibson} as an example of the extraordinary circumstances that defeat abstention). This ground is distinct from the due-process recusal question of \textit{Caperton v.\ A.T.\ Massey Coal Co.}, 556 U.S.\ 868 (2009): it asks not whether a particular ruling must be vacated for bias, but the antecedent abstention question whether any state forum entitled to deference exists at all. Here, it does not. Defendant Thompson---a named defendant in this action---has continued to adjudicate Plaintiff's cases while his own conduct is the subject of them. He initiated the contempt proceeding on Clark's ex parte communication without notice to Plaintiff; he denied Plaintiff's dispositive motion without counsel, without a State objection, and without a hearing, after representing on the record that nothing would be decided until counsel was appointed; and he denied Plaintiff's motion to appear remotely at the very hearing where the accuracy of his own on-the-record finding was at issue, ensuring Plaintiff could not correct it. Thompson has, moreover, prejudged the merits on the record. When Plaintiff stated that she had been misidentified---the factual core of her defense---Thompson answered, on the record on April 28, 2025, ``That doesn't matter.'' When Plaintiff insisted that it did, Thompson replied: ``I already pulled the cases on this. And I'm telling you the law is not in your favor.'' That is the very ``prejudice'' for which \sect\ 1983's federal forum was designed. \textit{Monroe v.\ Pape}, 365 U.S.\ 167, 174--80 (1961). A judge who has independently researched and decided a defendant's case off the record, before any hearing and before any briefing, and who dismisses the defendant's central exculpatory fact as immaterial, is not a neutral arbiter but an adversary. That is the biased tribunal to which \textit{Younger} deference does not extend. \textit{Gibson v.\ Berryhill}, 411 U.S.\ at 578--579. Presiding Judge Guptil, to whom the \textit{Roberts} objection was referred, revoked a bail that did not exist and issued a warrant for Plaintiff's failure to appear at a hearing Plaintiff had been prevented from attending, while entertaining the State's objection to a \textit{Roberts} dismissal---a dismissal established by a decision of the Oregon Supreme Court, not by any statute---an objection frivolous on the face of the record, its premise that Plaintiff ``fired'' two lawyers contradicted by the record and by Thompson's own finding that Lasher's withdrawal was a mandatory conflict of Lasher's own making. A tribunal that adjudicates the very conduct alleged against it, and that manufactures the predicates for its own coercive orders, is not the neutral forum whose authority \textit{Younger} protects. This Court owes it no deference. Reassignment or recusal does not cure this bias, and the State cannot invoke the availability of recusal as an adequate state remedy. As in \textit{Gibson}, where the disqualifying interest infected the entire tribunal so that no recusal could produce a neutral decisionmaker, the partiality here is not confined to a single judge whom Plaintiff might move to recuse. Plaintiff's matter already reached a second judge---Presiding Judge Guptil, the official responsible for reassignment---and that afforded no neutral forum: Guptil revoked a bail that did not exist, issued a failure-to-appear warrant from a hearing Plaintiff had been prevented from attending, and entertained the State's facially frivolous objection to a \textit{Roberts} dismissal---a remedy grounded in a decision of the Oregon Supreme Court rather than in any statute---resting on the false premise, contradicted by the record, that Plaintiff had ``fired'' her appointed counsel. Nor did Thompson's partiality remain in his own courtroom: it was Thompson who denied Plaintiff's motion to appear telephonically at the \textit{Roberts} hearing in Guptil's court, ensuring that Plaintiff could not be heard even before a different judge. A bias that follows Plaintiff from courtroom to courtroom, and that reaches into the proceedings of the very judge to whom reassignment would send her, is not remediable by recusal; it is the structural disqualification that \textit{Gibson} holds defeats \textit{Younger} deference. \textit{Gibson v.\ Berryhill}, 411 U.S.\ at 577--579.
	\end{paras}
	\begin{paras}
		
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item As to Plaintiff's claims seeking damages, \textit{Younger}
		abstention does not apply in any event.
		\textit{Deakins v.\ Monaghan}, 484 U.S.\ 193, 201--04 (1988).
		
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item \textit{Heck v.\ Humphrey}, 512 U.S.\ 477 (1994), does not bar
		Plaintiff's claims. Plaintiff's requests for declaratory and
		prospective injunctive relief are not barred by \textit{Heck}.
		\textit{Edwards v.\ Balisok}, 520 U.S.\ 641, 649 (1997). The
		unlawful home entry (Count III), First Amendment retaliation
		(Count IV), equal protection (Count V), familial association
		(Count XI), and unlawful child removal (Count XI) claims are
		independent constitutional torts whose success would not invalidate
		any future conviction. To the extent any claim is deemed subject to
		\textit{Heck}, Plaintiff requests that such claim be stayed, not
		dismissed. \textit{Deakins}, 484 U.S.\ at 201--04.
		
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item Plaintiff satisfies all four factors for a preliminary injunction under \textit{Winter v.\ Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.}, 555 U.S.\ 7, 20 (2008): (1) likelihood of success on the merits; (2) likelihood of irreparable harm in the absence of preliminary relief; (3) the balance of equities tips in Plaintiff's favor; and (4) an injunction is in the public interest.
		\textit{First}, Plaintiff is likely to succeed on the merits. The blank probable cause order in the State's own discovery production constitutes documentary proof of a \textit{Gerstein} violation. Rambin's December 27, 2024 supplemental report is a sworn official document in which the arresting officer states in writing that the September 17 suspect was not Plaintiff. The indictment was returned during the PJO gap, a jurisdictional defect confirmed in writing by the Washington County Trial Court Administrator. The May 10, 2025 home entry was warrantless and unsupported by any exigency. These facts are not contested; they appear in the State's own records. Likelihood of success is not merely plausible---it is established by the government's own documents.
		\textit{Second}, Plaintiff will suffer irreparable harm absent preliminary relief. Six independent harms are not compensable by damages: (a) Plaintiff's minor daughter was removed from her custody on September 6, 2025; developmental time lost to unlawful parent-child separation cannot be restored by a damages award, and access to her child is currently conditioned on submission to arrest under void warrants; (b) the \textit{Gerstein} violation is structurally incapable of post-conviction remedy; (c) two void warrants remain outstanding and create an imminent risk of seizure; (d) the OPDC placeholder creates a self-concealing record routing notices away from Plaintiff; (e) the demurrer and motion to quash have been pending more than twenty-one months and ORS 138.035 bars interlocutory appeal---the Court of Appeals confirmed on August 15, 2025 that no appellate jurisdiction exists; and (f) each day of continued prosecution under a void indictment is itself an irreparable constitutional injury. \textit{Betschart v.\ State of Oregon}, No.\ 23-2270, slip op.\ at 18 (9th Cir.\ May 31, 2024) (``[d]eprivation of constitutional rights `unquestionably constitutes irreparable injury'\thinspace'').
		
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item \textit{Third}, the balance of
		equities tips sharply in Plaintiff's favor. Defendants face no cognizable harm from being required to comply with the Constitution. The State has no legitimate interest in the prosecution it seeks to continue, for three reasons: the indictment is void; the arresting officer repudiated the arrest in writing; and the warrants now used to seize Plaintiff are themselves the product of the constitutional violations alleged in this Complaint. Against that absence of any legitimate state interest, Plaintiff faces ongoing separation from her child, imminent seizure on those warrants, and the permanent loss of her constitutional challenges through the appellate trap described above.
		\textit{Fourth}, the public interest favors injunctive relief.
		Federal courts have a ``virtually unflagging'' obligation to exercise
		the jurisdiction conferred on them. \textit{Colorado River Water
			Conservation Dist.\ v.\ United States}, 424 U.S.\ 800, 817 (1976).
		The public interest is served by ensuring that state criminal
		proceedings are grounded in lawful authority and that federal
		constitutional guarantees are not nullified by structural mechanisms
		that prevent any court from ever reviewing them.
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item Plaintiff's claims arising from Defendants' extension of the
		warrants into juvenile dependency proceeding 25JU04261---including
		use of the warrants to deny records access, obstruct filings,
		restrict parent-child contact, and defer adjudication pending
		Plaintiff's apprehension---are pled in Counts IX, X, and XI. Plaintiff
		does not ask this Court to adjudicate the custody or dependency
		merits; she seeks redress for the constitutional violations of the
		individual actors described herein.
		
	\end{paras}

	\noindent The fourteen claims that follow are not fourteen separate grievances. They are one constitutional error---Deputy Rambin's misidentification arrest of Plaintiff---and the successive acts by which Washington County and its officials sought to protect that error from correction. Each Count states an independent constitutional violation; read together, they establish the municipal custom of institutional containment alleged in Counts VI and XIII.

	\subsection*{Count I --- False Arrest in Violation of the Fourth Amendment\\
		(42 U.S.C.\ \S\ 1983 --- Against Rambin)}
	\begin{paras}
		
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item Plaintiff realleges and incorporates by reference all
		preceding paragraphs as though fully set forth herein.
		
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item The Fourth Amendment prohibits seizure of a person without
		a warrant or individualized probable cause.
		\textit{Illinois v.\ Gates}, 462 U.S.\ 213, 238 (1983).
		Probable cause must exist at the moment of arrest; it cannot
		be supplied by investigation conducted after the arrest.
		\textit{Devenpeck v.\ Alford}, 543 U.S.\ 146, 152 (2004).
		
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item At the time of Plaintiff's arrest on September 18, 2024,
		Rambin lacked a warrant and lacked individualized probable
		cause to believe Plaintiff had committed any offense. Rambin
		placed Plaintiff in handcuffs before Orozco had exited his
		vehicle, before any witness had been interviewed, and before
		any identification inquiry had been conducted. The
		investigation purporting to supply probable cause---Rambin's
		December 27, 2024 supplemental report---was filed three months
		after the arrest. Rambin's own report confirms the
		misidentification.
		
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item Rambin acted under color of state law. His conduct was not
		objectively reasonable. \textit{Pearson v.\ Callahan}, 555 U.S.\ 223, 231 (2009). Specifically: (a) the CAD narrative log establishes that Rambin entered 'ONE DETAINED' at 21:44 before Orozco had run Plaintiff's driver's license query; (b) the reports of Rambin, Orozco, and Stone contain a circular probable cause attribution in which each officer defers to the others and no officer articulates an individualized basis for concluding that the woman detained was the woman in the September 17 surveillance footage; (c) Rambin's own December 27, 2024 supplemental report acknowledges in writing that the September 17 suspect was not Plaintiff; (d) the probable cause affidavit exists in two versions with different dollar figures, was sworn before a law enforcement officer rather than a neutral magistrate, and was never filed with the court.
		
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item The show cause and contempt proceedings compounded these violations, and did so knowingly. As set forth above, the court imposed no release condition upon Plaintiff at her re-arraignment on the District Attorney's information, and Defendant Thompson---who presided over that re-arraignment---knew that no such condition had been imposed there. Thompson nonetheless initiated a show cause proceeding charging Plaintiff with violating a condition that did not exist---off the record, without notice to Plaintiff, and while Plaintiff was without counsel. Subjecting a person to the loss of her liberty for disobeying a directive the court knew it had never issued is not a procedural misstep; it is a knowing deprivation of liberty without due process. \textit{Mullane}, 339 U.S.\ at 314; \textit{Mathews}, 424 U.S.\ at 333. That deliberate use of a predicate Thompson knew to be nonexistent also confirms that the proceedings against Plaintiff were maintained in bad faith. \textit{Dombrowski v.\ Pfister}, 380 U.S.\ 479, 490 (1965).

		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item \textbf{Qualified Immunity.} Rambin is not entitled to
		qualified immunity. The right to be free from arrest without
		individualized probable cause was clearly established as of
		September 18, 2024. \textit{Illinois v.\ Gates}, 462 U.S.\ 213
		(1983); \textit{Beck v.\ Ohio}, 379 U.S.\ 89, 91 (1964). The
		rule that probable cause must exist at the moment of arrest,
		not be constructed after the fact, was clearly established.
		\textit{Devenpeck}, 543 U.S.\ at 152. The rule that racial
		proximity to a suspect, without more, does not supply
		individualized probable cause was clearly established.
		\textit{Ybarra v.\ Illinois}, 444 U.S.\ 85, 91 (1979). No
		reasonable deputy sheriff could have believed that handcuffing
		a person before any identification inquiry, based solely on
		her presence near another person of the same race, was
		constitutionally permissible.
		
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item As a direct and proximate result of this violation,
		Plaintiff suffered loss of liberty, physical and emotional
		harm, and damages in amounts to be proven at trial.
		
	\end{paras}

	\subsection*{Count III --- Unlawful Home Entry (42 U.S.C.\ \S\ 1983\\
		--- Against Thompson, Bineham, Clark, and Does 1--4)}
	\begin{paras}
		
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item Plaintiff realleges and incorporates by reference all
		preceding paragraphs as though fully set forth herein.
		
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item The Fourth Amendment prohibits warrantless entry into a
		private home absent a valid judicial warrant, consent, or
		exigent circumstances. \textit{Payton v.\ New York},
		445 U.S.\ 573, 589--90 (1980). A warrant authorizing arrest
		of a third party does not authorize entry into a home.
		\textit{Steagald v.\ United States}, 451 U.S.\ 204, 211--12
		(1981). An administrative order under ORS 144.350 is not a
		judicial warrant and does not authorize home entry.
		
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item On May 10, 2025, Does 1--4 entered Plaintiff's home
		without a valid judicial warrant, without consent, and without
		exigent circumstances, acting pursuant to a LEDS entry made
		by Bineham that mislabeled an ORS 144.350 administrative
		order as a ``felony warrant.'' Clark initiated the enforcement
		chain through the ex parte communication to Thompson.
		Thompson's ex parte direction to the release office to seek a
		violation report, outside any pending adversarial proceeding,
		was the proximate cause of the home entry. That direction is
		an administrative act to which absolute judicial immunity does
		not attach. \textit{Forrester v.\ White}, 484 U.S.\ 219,
		228 (1988).
		
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item \textbf{Qualified Immunity.} Defendants Thompson, Bineham,
		Clark, and Does 1--4 are not entitled to qualified immunity.
		The rule that a home may not be entered without a judicial
		warrant was clearly established as of May 10, 2025.
		\textit{Payton}, 445 U.S.\ at 589--90. The rule that an
		administrative order does not constitute a judicial warrant
		for purposes of home entry was clearly established.
		\textit{Steagald}, 451 U.S.\ at 211--12. The rule that
		judicial immunity does not attach to administrative acts was
		clearly established. \textit{Forrester}, 484 U.S.\ at 228.
		No reasonable officer or official could have believed that
		entry into a private home pursuant to an administrative
		order mislabeled in LEDS as a ``felony warrant'' was
		constitutionally permissible.
		
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item As a direct and proximate result of this violation,
		Plaintiff suffered invasion of her home, bail revocation, and
		the issuance of a current arrest warrant that is the direct
		fruit of the Fourth Amendment violation. Plaintiff suffered
		damages in amounts to be proven at trial.
		
	\end{paras}

	\subsection*{Count VI --- Monell Leg 1: Unconstitutional Custom of Relay-Chain Probable Cause\\
		(42 U.S.C.\ \S\ 1983 --- Against Washington County)}
	\begin{paras}
		
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item Plaintiff realleges and incorporates by reference all
		preceding paragraphs as though fully set forth herein.
		
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item Washington County, through the Washington County
		Sheriff's Office, maintained a custom or practice of
		effecting arrests based on relay-chain probable cause: one
		officer communicates a probable cause determination to
		another, who then effects an arrest without independent
		verification of the factual predicate.
		\textit{Monell v.\ Dep't of Soc.\ Servs.},
		436 U.S.\ 658, 690--91 (1978).
		
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item That custom was the moving force behind Plaintiff's
		arrest. Rambin communicated his probable cause determination
		to Orozco. Orozco effected the arrest---or was present while
		Rambin effected the arrest---without independently verifying
		whether Plaintiff was the individual depicted in the
		surveillance footage Rambin had collected two hours earlier.
		The relay-chain custom caused Plaintiff's arrest without
		individualized probable cause.
		
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item Washington County failed to train its deputies to require
		individualized probable cause before arrest and failed to
		supervise the relay-chain practice. That failure to train
		and supervise was the moving force behind the constitutional
		violation. \textit{City of Canton v.\ Harris}, 489 U.S.\ 378, 388--89 (1989).
		
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item Washington County also maintained three additional unconstitutional policies or customs that were each a moving force behind Plaintiff's constitutional injuries. First, Washington County maintained a policy or custom of failing to ensure that post-arrest judicial probable cause determinations were completed within the 36-hour ORS 133.145 window and the 48-hour \textit{McLaughlin} window. The blank probable cause order in the State's own discovery production---every judicial field empty---establishes that the failure was not an isolated incident but the product of a system that generated the required form without ensuring its completion. Second, the Washington County District Attorney's Office maintained a written policy prohibiting more than one DDA from having an open case on a single defendant. That policy was violated when DDA Stabler filed in 24CR27775 while DDA Gerten remained on 24CR49043. The violation resulted in a DDA with incomplete knowledge of the record making false representations to the court about Plaintiff's conduct. Third, the Washington County District Attorney's Office maintained a written policy providing that cases with fewer than three witnesses should not be routed to the grand jury. Plaintiff's case had two witnesses. The DA's Office routed it to the grand jury anyway, in violation of its own policy, during the PJO gap, producing a void indictment. Each of these policy violations was a moving force behind the constitutional deprivations Plaintiff suffered.
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item As a direct and proximate result of this custom,
		Plaintiff suffered the constitutional violations described
		herein and damages in amounts to be proven at trial.
		
	\end{paras}

	\subsection*{Count XIV --- Sixth and Fourteenth Amendments: Denial of Counsel at Critical Stages Without Valid Waiver\\
		(42 U.S.C.\ \S\ 1983 --- Against Washington County)}
	\begin{paras}
		
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item Plaintiff realleges and incorporates by reference all preceding paragraphs as though fully set forth herein.
		
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item The Sixth Amendment, applied to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, guarantees the assistance of counsel at every critical stage of a criminal proceeding. \textit{Gideon v.\ Wainwright}, 372 U.S.\ 335 (1963). That right is not forfeited by silence. A defendant may proceed without counsel only upon a waiver that is knowing, intelligent, and voluntary, and the court bears an affirmative duty to ensure that any such waiver is valid. \textit{Johnson v.\ Zerbst}, 304 U.S.\ 458 (1938). The right of self-representation likewise arises only where a defendant ``voluntarily and intelligently'' elects to forgo counsel; competence to self-represent is not the question, election is. \textit{Faretta v.\ California}, 422 U.S.\ 806 (1975). The complete denial of counsel at a critical stage is structural error, not subject to harmless-error review. \textit{United States v.\ Cronic}, 466 U.S.\ 648, 659 (1984).
		
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item On April 28, 2025, following the withdrawal of Plaintiff's appointed counsel, the Washington County Circuit Court represented on the record that Plaintiff would be provided new counsel, that all future hearing dates were cancelled, and that no decision would be made until counsel was appointed. Plaintiff expressly declined to represent herself. Plaintiff never thereafter waived her right to counsel, and never elected to proceed pro se.
		
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item Notwithstanding that the County's own records reflected that Plaintiff had no counsel of record other than the OPDC placeholder that never appeared on her behalf, the State proceeded against Plaintiff at critical stages while she remained unrepresented and without any valid waiver. In particular, the hearing on the State's objection to Plaintiff's \textit{Roberts} dismissal in 24CR27775 went forward while the State and the court knew Plaintiff was without counsel; Plaintiff was thereby required to litigate it pro se; her release was revoked and a failure-to-appear warrant issued against her. Additional adverse hearings proceeded on the same footing. At no point did any court obtain from Plaintiff the knowing, intelligent, and voluntary waiver that \textit{Johnson v.\ Zerbst} and \textit{Faretta} require. Plaintiff's competence to represent herself is immaterial; the constitutional defect is the absence of any waiver at all.
		
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item The same denial recurred, one step earlier, in the June 2025 show cause proceeding. That proceeding---initiated after Defendant Thompson's ex parte communication to the release office, and noticed only to a residential address Plaintiff had not occupied for years and to the OPDC placeholder that never appeared---advanced against Plaintiff while she was both unrepresented and unnotified, and produced a warrant for her arrest. The show cause charged Plaintiff with violating a release condition that had never been imposed upon her at her re-arraignment on the District Attorney's information---a fact within Defendant Thompson's own knowledge. Whether a behavioral condition from an earlier release silently carries forward to a later charging instrument without being specifically re-imposed is a question of Oregon criminal procedure that no unrepresented lay person could be expected to identify or contest, and precisely the function that counsel serves. The absence of counsel was therefore not harmless: it left Plaintiff to face a contempt-type proceeding, and the loss of her liberty, on a predicate that competent counsel would have challenged as never imposed and never communicated to her.
		
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item This was not an isolated lapse but the operation of a known, systemic failure. In \textit{Betschart v.\ State of Oregon}, No.\ 23-2270, slip op.\ at 17--18 (9th Cir.\ May 31, 2024), the Ninth Circuit found that Washington County---the same county, and as to its Circuit Court judges and Sheriff the same defendants named here---maintained a system that left pretrial defendants without the counsel to which they were constitutionally entitled. Plaintiff's experience is a continuation of that documented systemic failure.
		
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item Washington County is liable under \textit{Monell v.\ Dep't of Soc.\ Servs.}, 436 U.S.\ 658, 690--91 (1978). The County maintained a custom and practice of entering a placeholder appointment (OPDC) that never appears, and then permitting liberty-affecting proceedings to go forward against defendants the County knows to be unrepresented and who have not waived counsel. \textit{Betschart} confirms the persistent and systemic character of that custom. It was the moving force behind the denial of counsel described above.
		
		\stepcounter{complaintpara}\item For this Count, Plaintiff seeks declaratory and prospective relief and damages against Washington County; she seeks no relief against any judicial officer in an individual capacity for any judicial act. As a direct and proximate result of the County's custom, Plaintiff was deprived of counsel at critical stages, her release was revoked and a warrant issued against her while she stood unrepresented, and she suffered the loss of liberty and the injuries described in this Complaint, in amounts to be proven at trial.
	\end{paras}
