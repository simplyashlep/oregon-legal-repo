A lack of a founded disposition is highly material because it limits what ODHS may truthfully claim its historical CPS records establish, bears directly on the reliability and weight of the evidence offered to prove jurisdiction, and exposes whether ODHS converted unverified allegations into asserted facts. It does not automatically bar jurisdiction—but it defeats an agency shortcut: treating intake history, reports, and “concerns” as proof of abuse or neglect.



The two standards

The court and agency operate under different legal frameworks.



Decision	Decisionmaker	Required finding	Standard

CPS assessment disposition	ODHS	Whether there is reasonable cause to believe abuse occurred	Reasonable cause

Juvenile jurisdiction	Court	Whether the child’s condition/circumstances pose a current threat of serious loss or injury reasonably likely to occur	Preponderance of the evidence

Emergency protective custody	ODHS / court process	Whether there is reasonable cause, based on specific and articulable facts, for imminent severe harm / statutory predicate	Reasonable cause; specific and articulable facts

A CPS founded disposition means ODHS determined there was reasonable cause to believe abuse occurred. An unfounded disposition means ODHS determined there was no reasonable cause. An unable-to-determine disposition means ODHS could not determine whether reasonable cause existed. The CPS worker must determine a disposition and explain the basis for it.



For dependency jurisdiction, ORS 419B.100(1)(c) requires the court to decide whether the child’s condition or circumstances endanger welfare. Oregon appellate law requires ODHS to prove by a preponderance that, considering the record as a whole, there is a current threat of serious loss or injury that is reasonably likely to be realized—not merely an old accusation, generalized concern, or possibility.



The court may be right in one narrow sense: lack of a founded disposition does not prevent the court from independently making findings on evidence presented at trial. But that is not the end of the question.
